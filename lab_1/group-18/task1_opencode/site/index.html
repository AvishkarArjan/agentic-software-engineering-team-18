Meet the team

Four people, one shared document, and a deeply held belief that the bug is always in the other branch.

We are four humans who met in the Agentic Software Engineering lab at Paderborn University and discovered a shared love of small diffs, strong coffee, and asking an agent to do the boring part. Between us we study compilers, data science, distributed systems, and why the build only breaks on Fridays. Our motto: if the tests pass, we celebrate; if they fail, we rename a variable and try again.

Shared view

The Future of Software Engineering

We asked an AI to predict the future. It said “it depends” and invoiced us for the tokens.

Here is the part where we pretend to be futurists. We do not agree on everything, but we do agree on this: AI is changing how software gets made, and the engineer's job is moving from typing to judgement. In this lab we watched an agent build a website and then immediately asked it to explain itself. That is basically the job now.

1. First drafts get cheap

That is the point — and the trap. A cheap draft is only useful if someone still reads it, questions it, and decides whether it belongs in the codebase.

2. The bottleneck moves to judgement

Specifying, reviewing, testing and documenting matter more, not less. The hard part is no longer typing the function; it is knowing which function deserves to exist.

3. Prompting is not engineering by itself

Understanding systems, data and failure modes is still the job. An agent can write the code, but it cannot own the consequences of a bad design decision.

4. Humans stay accountable

The best teams use agents for the boring parts and keep humans responsible for decisions that are hard to reverse. “The AI did it” is not an incident report.

One thing we don't agree on

Will most code still be written by hand in ten years?

The optimists

No. Agents will do most of the typing, and engineers will review, design and debug the weird stuff. We will look back at manual boilerplate the way we look at assembly language.

The sceptics

Mostly yes. The hard part of software is not typing; it is deciding what to build and why. Agents will write more code, but humans will still own the thinking — and the 3 a.m. incident call.

We agree to disagree, then ask the agent to write a test for both opinions. It passes. Suspicious.
